## Overview

When I joined Fiverr, "the design system" was a Photoshop UI toolkit — a shared file of styles, not a system. Over the following years, as Director of Product Design, I drove the effort to turn that into Penta: a real, team-owned design system, built and maintained by a dedicated team in partnership with engineering.

This is a leadership story more than a hands-on one. I identified the need, made the business case for the investment, created the design-system team, and set the direction. The hands-on build was led by a dedicated product designer working alongside a four-person engineering team — my role was to establish the function, secure the buy-in, and keep it aligned with where the product was going.

## The Problem

A design system isn't a style guide — a style guide is one part of it. Fiverr had the style guide but not the system, and the gaps were starting to cost us.

**No design-system authority.** There was no single owner and no agreed source of truth, so consistency depended on individual designers remembering how things were "supposed" to be done.

**No shared language.** Designers, writers, and engineers described the same components in different terms. A name that meant something in a design file meant nothing in the codebase — so intent got lost in translation on the way to production.

**A widening gap between design and production.** What designers specced and what actually shipped drifted apart, because there was no systematic way to keep them in sync.

**Accumulating tech debt.** Without shared, reusable components, every new feature rebuilt what already existed — slower to ship, harder to maintain, and steadily eroding the level of experience we were trying to hold.

Underneath all of it: it was becoming hard to maintain the quality of experience we wanted, and the design organization was scaling faster than the system holding it together.

## Making the Case

Building a design system is expensive. It needs a dedicated designer, a team of engineers, and a lot of ongoing effort — so before any of it could happen, the investment had to be justified to management.

Consistency alone wasn't the argument that would land. The one that did was velocity: with a shared library of ready-made components and patterns on both the design and development sides, teams could build new features and flows far faster — instead of rebuilding the same pieces from scratch every time. Framing Penta as a way to ship faster, not just look more consistent, was what turned it from a design preference into a business decision worth funding.

Making that case wasn't a solo effort. My manager, the Head of Design, helped build the argument — including bringing in an outside expert to speak to the value of a design system, giving leadership external validation alongside the internal push. Securing buy-in for an investment this size is its own kind of work, separate from the design itself — and it's what made everything that followed possible.

## The Approach

With the investment secured, the build came down to five moves.

**Create a design-system team.** The first and most important decision was that a design system needs an owner. I established a dedicated team and appointed a product designer to own Penta — turning "everyone's responsibility" (which meant no one's) into a clear mandate, backed by engineering.

**Build it from the ground up.** Rather than patch the existing toolkit, the team rebuilt the system properly — components, patterns, and the structure to hold them — documenting as it went so the system was legible from day one.

**Create a shared language.** The team closed the gap between how design named things and how engineering implemented them, so a component meant the same thing on both sides. This alignment is what makes a design system actually reduce work rather than add a translation layer — and it's what delivers on the velocity the whole case was built on.

**Build it together.** Penta was built as a partnership between design and engineering, with a contribution model that let people across both disciplines take part in improving it — so it became a shared asset the organization owned, not a library handed down from one team.

**Document everything.** Everything lived in a single, accessible place — guidelines, examples, and onboarding material — so new designers and engineers could get started, catch up, and contribute without needing someone to explain the system to them.

## Outcome

Over several years, Penta went from a Photoshop toolkit to a team-owned system with a dedicated designer, an engineering partnership, a shared design-to-code language, and full documentation — the foundation the product design organization scaled on.

But the part I'm proudest of sits upstream of the system itself: recognizing that something this expensive had to be sold on velocity, making that case to leadership, and building the team to deliver on it. Identifying a systemic problem, securing the investment to fix it, and setting the direction while the people closest to the work did the building — that's the work a design director does that never shows up in a single screen.

## What I'd Do Differently

We treated the design system as something to invest in once its absence became painful — once the tech debt, the drift, and the missing shared language had already accumulated. In hindsight, the case for a dedicated system and owner could have been made earlier, before the gaps became the bottleneck. It's a lesson I've carried forward: a design system is a strategic foundation, and the argument for it — the velocity, the reduced duplication — is usually true long before anyone feels the pain of not having one.
