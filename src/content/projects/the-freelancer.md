## Overview & Context

Fiverr's marketplace is built around gigs: discrete, highly specific services that a seller creates and lists individually. If you're a graphic designer, you don't create a listing that says "I'm a graphic designer." You create separate listings — one for logo design, one for photo restoration, one for photo manipulation — each with its own pricing tiers (basic, standard, premium), its own deliverables, file formats, revision count, turnaround time, and Q&A.

The model works well for buyers: they see exactly what they're getting and what it costs. But for sellers it creates a real problem. A skilled graphic designer might be capable of twenty or thirty or forty different things. Nobody creates forty gigs. It's too much to set up and too much to maintain. So most sellers create a handful of their most marketable gigs, and the rest of what they can do simply isn't visible on the platform.

The result is a systematic underrepresentation of seller capability — and a missed opportunity for buyers who can't find what they need.

## The Idea

The concept was to decouple the seller's profile from the gig creation process. Instead of building out full service listings, a seller would simply:

1. **Add skills and samples** — show what they can do through examples of their work (photo restoration, 3D model building, illustration, video editing, etc.) with minimal setup
2. **Set a rate** — a single hourly rate that the seller keeps private
3. **Let Fiverr price it** — for any given skill, Fiverr calculates the likely cost based on how long that type of work typically takes, multiplied by the seller's rate. The buyer sees a starting price; the seller's rate stays behind the scenes.

If a piece of work takes ten hours and the seller charges sixty dollars an hour, the buyer sees a six hundred dollar starting price — without either side having to negotiate that from scratch. For more complex or unusual briefs, the custom offer flow (already familiar to both sides) handles the edge cases.

## Outcome

This was a concept — it never went into production. But it identified a genuine tension in how Fiverr's gig model works for sellers at scale: the more capable and versatile a freelancer is, the worse the current model represents them. The Freelancer explored what it might look like to fix that — making it easy for sellers to show everything they can do, while keeping the buyer experience clean, transactional, and familiar.
